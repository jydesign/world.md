Warren and Mary's farm is a place built from use rather than comfort. It feeds,
shelters, and employs, but it also judges. Every part of it carries the pressure
of labor: the barn, the porch, the meadow, the upper pasture, the paths between
house and outbuilding. It is not a symbolic countryside detached from hardship.
It is the material world in which usefulness, dignity, and human obligation are
measured.

Because the farm is the place Silas returns to, it holds more than physical
shelter. It becomes the testing ground for what "home" means. It is where
resentment, pity, memory, and duty all converge. The farm must therefore feel
both protective and severe: a place that can receive someone in the end, but
only under the shadow of what has gone unresolved.
