An elderly hired man returns to the farm he previously abandoned during
haying season. While he sleeps inside, Mary tries to persuade her husband
Warren to receive him not as unreliable labor, but as a dying man who has
chosen their farm as his final home. Warren moves reluctantly toward mercy,
only to discover that Silas has died before anything can be repaired.

The central question is whether home is a place earned through loyalty and
contribution, or the place where a person may be received when there is
nothing left to earn. Its themes — labor and human worth, mercy that need not
be deserved, homecoming, age, guilt, and the limits of reconciliation — stay
inside the ordinary domestic world: thresholds and doorways, weak lamplight
surrounded by rural darkness, hands carrying and reaching, unfinished work,
warmth that cannot prevent death.

This is a story about a man who returns to the only place he can approach
without admitting that he needs to be taken in. Silas calls his return work
because work is the last form of dignity he understands. Mary recognizes the
truth beneath his plans. Warren recognizes the history beneath his need.

The farm holds both truths at once. Silas has failed Warren, and Silas is
dying. Warren has reason to refuse him, and refusal would still be a moral
choice. Mary does not erase the past. She insists that the present condition
of another human being must also be seen.

The story ends before mercy can become reconciliation. Warren reaches the
point of action only to discover that no action remains. Home receives Silas,
but not through a clean decision, an apology, or a restored relationship. It
receives him through death.
