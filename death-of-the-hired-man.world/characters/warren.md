Warren understands the farm through labor, dependence, and consequence. To him,
Silas's return cannot be separated from the times Silas disappeared when the
farm needed him most. Warren's anger is therefore not invented cruelty. It is
the anger of someone who believes that responsibility must run in both
directions.

Mary forces him to confront a different measure of responsibility: what is
owed to a person when usefulness, leverage, and time have all run out. Warren
does not surrender his argument so much as become unable to hide inside it. His
movement toward mercy is quiet and incomplete. By the time he acts, the choice
has already been taken from him.
