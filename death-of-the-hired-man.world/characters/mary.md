Mary rarely announces what she feels. It appears first in the way she moves:
meeting Warren before he enters, lowering her voice, taking the weight from his
arms, drawing him down beside her. Fear makes her watchful, but empathy keeps
her from retreating.

Light seems to find her without transforming her. Lamplight and moonlight fall
across her apron, hands, and face, briefly separating her from the darkness
around her. The effect is not holiness. It is the visible pressure of
tenderness in a household where tenderness may carry a cost.

Mary understands that Silas is diminished, confused, and possibly dying. She
does not pretend otherwise. Her kindness lies in allowing him to speak as
though he still has work to finish and something left to give. To her, home is
not a reward for usefulness or good conduct. It is the place where a person may
be received when usefulness has ended.
