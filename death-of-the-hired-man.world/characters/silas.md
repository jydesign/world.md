Silas returns speaking the language available to him: work. He cannot say that
he is homeless, frightened, dying, or ashamed. He can say that the meadow
should be ditched, the upper pasture cleared, and a younger man taught how to
build hay properly.

His plans are not simply delusions. They are the final shape taken by his need
to matter. He wants to leave evidence that he possessed knowledge worth passing
on and that his life was more than a succession of departures, temporary
wages, and winter returns.

Silas's tragedy is not that no one understands him. Mary understands him almost
completely. The tragedy is that understanding arrives when there is no work,
apology, lesson, or reconciliation left to perform.
